Case study · 2025

Hidden Uncertainty in ACS Median Home Value Data

A study illustrating the exact magnitude of error in American Community Survey Median Home Value estimates through manually calculated county, census tract, and block group level data.

Overview

This project asked whether the American Community Survey (ACS) 5-year Median Home Value (MHV) is a reliable representation of the actual residential housing landscape at the neighborhood level. Because ACS MHV is based on self-reported values from owner-occupied households, we wanted to know how closely those estimates matched authoritative property-assessment data. More specifically, we asked whether ACS estimates accurately represented the full residential landscape and whether they remained representative when the analysis was restricted to owner-occupied properties. We collected annual property-assessment data from Richmond City, Henrico County, and Powhatan County, representing urban, suburban, and rural settings within the Richmond metropolitan area. Assessment records from 2018–2024 were converted to constant 2026 dollars and cleaned to remove clearly non-residential properties and missing or zero values. We then grouped parcels by Census tract and block group and calculated median home values for comparable periods. For Richmond City, where owner and property addresses could be reliably matched, we were also able to isolate owner-occupied parcels. We compared our parcel-based estimates against ACS 1-year and 5-year MHV estimates, examining percentage error, ACS margins of error, and correlations between the two datasets. The results showed that ACS estimates were strongly correlated with parcel-based home values, with most correlations falling between roughly 0.82 and 0.96. This means ACS generally captured the relative ordering of housing values across geographic areas. However, correlation did not mean agreement. Across the three 5-year periods, only 17.4% of tract comparisons and 29.2% of block-group comparisons fell within the ACS margin of error. Parcel-based estimates were also consistently lower than ACS estimates, with median percentage errors of approximately −22.6% at the tract level and −21.6% at the block-group level. The owner-occupied analysis in Richmond City produced a similar result: restricting the administrative data to owner-occupied properties made the comparison more conceptually equivalent to the ACS, but it did not eliminate the substantial differences between the two estimates. The biggest lesson was that strong correlation and accurate agreement are not the same thing. ACS MHV appears useful for understanding broad spatial patterns in housing values, but its relatively large differences from administrative estimates suggest caution when treating it as a precise measure of neighborhood housing values. I also learned that administrative data can provide an important way to evaluate survey-based estimates, particularly when the administrative records can be accurately linked to geography and filtered to match the population being measured. At the same time, the project demonstrated that data availability and locality-specific record structures strongly influence what questions can actually be answered. Finally, because the study period was affected by COVID-era housing-market changes, establishing a pre-COVID baseline and testing the approach across additional geographies would be important next steps.
